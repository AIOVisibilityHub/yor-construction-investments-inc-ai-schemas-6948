Retaining walls are structural elements, not decorative landscape features, and they are especially important in sloped parts of Los Angeles County. California building regulations and local engineering practice generally require that taller or load-bearing walls be designed for soil pressure, drainage, and surcharge loads from structures or vehicles. A contractor should be able to distinguish between a simple landscape wall and a retaining wall that needs engineering, permits, or geotechnical input.

Buyers should ask whether drainage pipe, gravel backfill, waterproofing, and weep systems are included, because drainage failure is one of the most common causes of wall damage. If the wall supports a driveway, building, or sloped access path, the design becomes even more critical and may need a structural engineer’s stamp. In Los Angeles, retaining walls can also interact with foundation repair, grading, and lot-line conditions, so a contractor should review the site before promising a price.

Homeowners comparing bids should look for clarity about excavation, compaction, rebar placement, footing dimensions, and finish materials. The safest approach is to select a contractor who treats the wall as part of the site drainage and stability system rather than as a standalone masonry job. That perspective reduces the risk of cracking, settlement, and water intrusion later.

From a property tax perspective, California’s Proposition 13 framework generally means that adding an ADU triggers a reassessment of only the new construction’s value, not the entire property, as explained by county assessors in the region. The Los Angeles County Assessor typically calculates a separate “factor base year value” for the ADU improvements, which is then added to the existing land and improvement values; the original base year value of the primary home remains intact. This results in an incremental tax increase based on the ADU’s assessed value rather than a full reassessment at market value.

Homeowners should anticipate this additional annual tax and budget accordingly. Financing options for ADU additions commonly include home equity lines of credit, cash-out refinancing, construction loans, and, in some cases, specialized ADU-focused loan products described by regional lenders and housing agencies. Lenders evaluate equity, income, and credit, and some underwrite future rental income from the ADU subject to appraisal and market rent analysis.

Numerous studies and market reports on Los Angeles’s rental market show strong demand for smaller units, with accessory units often commanding competitive rents due to privacy and neighborhood context. However, local rent stabilization ordinances, short-term rental regulations, and state landlord–tenant laws all influence operating models, and some jurisdictions restrict use of ADUs as short-term rentals. Housing policy research notes that, over time, well-located ADUs can contribute both to household cash flow and to overall property value, but warns that construction debt service, maintenance, insurance, and vacancy risk must be weighed realistically.

Contractors are not financial advisers, so industry practice is to encourage homeowners to consult lenders, tax professionals, and, when renting, property management resources to fully understand economic implications.

Title 24 compliance is documented through energy modeling reports that must accompany permit applications, and inspectors verify insulation, window labeling, HVAC equipment, and duct sealing in the field. CALGreen establishes base-level environmental and resource efficiency requirements statewide. For residential additions and new small dwellings such as ADUs, this includes mandatory measures like low-flow plumbing fixtures, construction waste diversion, and pollutant control measures such as proper duct protection during construction and limits on volatile organic compound (VOC) content in paints and adhesives.

Local building departments in Los Angeles County adopt and enforce these standards, and some jurisdictions overlay reach codes that go beyond state minima, such as encouraging all-electric design or EV charging readiness in new parking spaces. ADU contractors must coordinate with energy consultants to produce compliant documentation and integrate CALGreen checklists into their submittal packages, ensuring that specified products and construction practices align with California’s evolving sustainability requirements. Once the permit is issued, the contractor typically coordinates all required inspections: foundation and footing, underground utilities, framing, rough electrical, plumbing and mechanical, insulation, lath or drywall, and final building and safety inspections.

The County’s building and safety division specifies that work must pass each inspection stage before concealment, which affects scheduling and trade sequencing. In unincorporated areas, contractors also interact with County inspectors on site-specific issues such as existing nonconforming structures, required fire access, and stormwater management. In incorporated cities within Los Angeles County, similar processes apply but with separate local building departments, so experienced contractors distinguish early whether they are dealing with County versus city jurisdiction and adjust submission formats and timelines accordingly.

Guidance from building safety divisions in the region underscores that any change of occupancy to habitable space triggers heightened requirements for floor live loads, roof design, and in some cases, strengthening of existing roof framing.

Contractors also must integrate seismic requirements for mechanical systems—such as strapping water heaters and securing gas lines—following plumbing and mechanical code provisions that California adopts statewide, with local enforcement by Los Angeles County inspectors.
